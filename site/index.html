Where this comes from

Signalling by smoke and fire is one of the oldest communication technologies there is, and it belongs to no single culture. Along the Great Wall of China, beacon towers passed word faster than any rider could carry it. Polybius described a Greek torch telegraph in the second century BC that could spell out arbitrary messages — two walls of torches and a codebook. Aboriginal Australian peoples used smoke to signal presence and intention across great distances, and it remains meaningful today. Many Native American nations used smoke signalling, and that heritage is living, not historical.

SMOKE draws on that worldwide history with respect and without costume. You will find no feathers here, no teepees, no invented “Indian” names, and no caricature. The app carries a history page crediting all of it, because the research is the interesting part.

How it works

Your smoke travels at about 20 mph. It is routed cell by cell across the continental United States using live forecasts from the National Weather Service, and it goes around storms rather than through them. A message to the next town takes an hour. A message across the country takes days.

Longer messages take longer to send, because the fire has to puff them out one breath at a time. This encourages brevity, which is the polite word for what it does.
